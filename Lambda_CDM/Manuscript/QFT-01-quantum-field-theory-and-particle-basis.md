# Chapter QFT-1: Quantum Field Theory and the Standard Model in Cosmology

In the $\Lambda\text{CDM}$ paradigm, General Relativity dictates the macroscopic geometry of spacetime, while the actual contents of the universe—matter, radiation, and vacuum energy—are governed by **Quantum Field Theory (QFT)** and the **Standard Model of Particle Physics**. 

Cosmology relies on QFT to determine the initial conditions of the universe, the interactions of matter and radiation in the early plasma, and the theoretical nature of dark energy.

## Fields and Quanta

In QFT, the universe is permeated by continuous quantum fields. The particles we observe are quantized excitations (quanta) of these underlying fields. The Standard Model categorizes these into two distinct classes based on their spin and statistical behavior:

1. **Fermions (Matter):** Particles with half-integer spin that obey Fermi-Dirac statistics and the Pauli exclusion principle. They include quarks (which form protons and neutrons) and leptons (such as electrons and neutrinos). In the cosmological energy budget, these contribute to the baryonic matter density ($\Omega_b$).
2. **Bosons (Forces):** Particles with integer spin that obey Bose-Einstein statistics and mediate fundamental forces. These include photons (electromagnetism), gluons (strong nuclear force), and the $W$ and $Z$ bosons (weak nuclear force). Photons dominate the radiation density parameter ($\Omega_r$) in the early universe.

## QFT in the Early Universe

Standard cosmology models the early universe as a hot, dense plasma where quantum field interactions dictate cosmic evolution. Key QFT-driven epochs include:

* **Cosmic Inflation:** An initial period of exponential expansion driven by a hypothetical scalar field (the inflaton). The macroscopic structures we observe today (galaxies and clusters) are theorized to originate from microscopic quantum fluctuations in this field that were stretched to cosmic scales.
* **Thermal Freeze-Out:** As the universe expanded and cooled, interaction rates between particles dropped below the expansion rate ($H$). This caused specific particle species to decouple from the thermal bath. This mechanism is responsible for the Cosmic Neutrino Background and the relic abundance of primordial elements (Big Bang Nucleosynthesis).

## The Vacuum State and the Cosmological Constant Problem

In classical physics, a vacuum is completely empty. In QFT, the vacuum is the lowest energy state of a field, but it is not zero. Quantum mechanics requires fields to undergo zero-point fluctuations. 

The zero-point energy for a harmonic oscillator is $\frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega$. To find the total vacuum energy density ($\rho_{\text{vac}}$), QFT integrates the zero-point energies of all field modes up to a cutoff scale (typically the Planck mass, $M_{\text{pl}} \approx 10^{19} \text{ GeV}$):

$$\rho_{\text{vac(theory)}} = \int_0^{k_{\text{max}}} \frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3} \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{k^2 + m^2} \approx \frac{M_{\text{pl}}^4}{8\pi^2} \approx 10^{74} \text{ GeV}^4$$

In $\Lambda\text{CDM}$, dark energy is modeled as the cosmological constant ($\Lambda$), representing the actual vacuum energy density of space. Observations from Type Ia supernovae and the CMB yield an empirical value of:

$$\rho_{\Lambda\text{(obs)}} \approx 10^{-47} \text{ GeV}^4$$

The ratio between the theoretical QFT prediction and the cosmological observation is approximately $10^{120}$. In mainstream physics, this discrepancy is known as the **Cosmological Constant Problem**, and it remains one of the major unsolved challenges in standard cosmological theory.
